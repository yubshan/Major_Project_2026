ABSTRACT
===============================================================================

Search-and-rescue operations in collapsed or otherwise hazardous environments are time-critical,
uncertain, and dangerous for human responders. Project Drishya investigates a modular autonomous
ground-robot framework intended to map an unknown environment, detect possible victims, navigate
toward confirmed targets, enforce deterministic safety rules, and explain each selected action.
The project proposal introduced a layered hybrid architecture combining Behavior Trees (BT),
Reinforcement Learning (RL), Genetic Algorithms (GA), and an optional Game Theory layer. During
the first development phase, the project scope was refined into four practical modules connected
through a shared thread-safe Blackboard: WiFi Channel State Information (CSI)-based human
detection, navigation and occupancy mapping, explainable decision logic, and simulation and
visualization. The use of WiFi CSI extends the original perception plan by targeting contactless,
non-line-of-sight human-presence sensing, while the original multi-hazard and explainable-control
objectives remain the long-term system context.

At the mid-term cutoff, the verified implementation includes a common 50 x 50 coordinate system,
sensor-packet validation, thread-safe shared state, mock sensor and detection generators, Bresenham
ray casting, ultrasonic and 8 x 8 Time-of-Flight (ToF) conversion, occupancy-grid updates, a
Gymnasium-compatible exploration environment, a six-stage priority Behavior Tree, bounded motor
commands, structured decision traces, and a headless navigation-decision integration harness.
The Behavior Tree provides fail-safe mission and data validation, proximity-based emergency stop,
confidence-gated victim confirmation, waypoint-directed steering, exploration using either a PPO
policy or deterministic heuristic fallback, and an idle state. A total of 97 automated tests pass
in the audited development environment: 56 tests for shared navigation/mapping functionality and
41 tests for decision nodes, tree priority, safety behavior, and the RL environment. The integration
harness also demonstrates idle, exploration, emergency-stop, and victim-navigation scenarios.

The present results prove modular software behavior under deterministic mock inputs; they do not
yet demonstrate rescue success rate, trained-policy superiority, WiFi CSI detection accuracy,
multi-hazard robustness, or hardware performance. No trained PPO checkpoint, GA optimizer,
integrated CSI pipeline, complete A* planner, complete four-panel simulator, ESP32 firmware, or
hardware-in-the-loop experiment is present in the audited repository. Accordingly, proposal values
such as 91.8% rescue success, 98.2-second execution time, Raspberry Pi operation at 42 Hz, and
ESP32 sensing at 100 Hz are treated as unverified targets and are not reported as mid-term results.

The next phase will focus on contract consolidation, closed-loop simulation, real WiFi CSI data
collection and baseline modeling, path planning, reproducible PPO-versus-heuristic experiments,
multi-hazard scenario design, hardware integration, and metric-based evaluation. The mid-term
outcome is therefore a tested and explainable software foundation, not a completed autonomous
rescue robot.

Keywords: Search and Rescue Robot, Behavior Tree, Explainable AI, Reinforcement Learning,
WiFi CSI, Occupancy Grid, Sensor Fusion, Autonomous Navigation, Disaster Robotics.

1. INTRODUCTION
===============================================================================

1.1 Background

Disaster sites such as collapsed buildings expose rescuers to unstable structures, poor visibility,
toxic substances, fire, and constrained passages. Search speed matters, but unverified autonomy can
introduce new risk. A rescue robot must therefore combine perception, mapping, navigation, safety,
and understandable decision-making rather than optimize only one task.

Conventional cameras depend on visibility, while infrared sensing may be limited by occlusion and
thermal conditions. WiFi CSI provides fine-grained information about how individual wireless
subcarriers are affected by multipath propagation, attenuation, and movement. Research has shown
that CSI can support device-free human sensing, including difficult non-line-of-sight settings,
although performance is strongly affected by wall material, geometry, interference, device
placement, and environmental change [7-9]. Consequently, WiFi CSI is treated in this project as a
promising perception modality requiring controlled experimental validation, not as guaranteed
"vision through concrete."

The decision problem is similarly complex. Purely rule-based control is understandable and can
enforce safety, but may be inflexible in unseen situations. A learned policy may adapt through
experience, but can be difficult to audit and cannot be allowed to bypass emergency constraints.
Project Drishya addresses this tension through a layered approach: deterministic Behavior Tree
branches own safety and mission priority; a learned or heuristic policy proposes exploration
actions; and every final decision is recorded in a structured trace.

1.2 Problem Statement

The engineering problem is to develop an affordable autonomous SAR robot that can incrementally
perceive an unknown environment, locate a possible trapped person without relying solely on direct
visibility, move toward valid targets without collision, and expose a human-readable and
machine-readable reason for each selected action. The system must continue to fail safely when
sensor data are absent, malformed, stale, or contradictory.

The current project is not yet a field-ready rescue system. The mid-term problem is narrower and
measurable: establish reliable software contracts and demonstrate that mapping and decision modules
behave correctly under controlled simulated inputs, while preparing the experimental path toward
closed-loop simulation and hardware.

1.3 Motivation

The project is motivated by three needs:

1. Responder safety: remote sensing and mapping may reduce unnecessary exposure of rescuers.
2. Search efficiency: autonomous exploration and non-visual human sensing may help prioritize
   areas for investigation.
3. Trust and auditability: rescue personnel need to know whether a stop or movement originated
   from operator control, a safety rule, a confirmed target, a learned policy, or a fallback.

1.4 Original Objectives from the Proposal

The proposal specified the following objectives:

1. Design a modular grid-based multi-hazard simulation and an affordable Raspberry Pi/ESP32 robot.
2. Integrate Behavior Trees for deterministic safety, RL for adaptive navigation, and GA for
   strategy optimization.
3. Develop an explainability module that maps actions to architectural layers in real time.

The optional Game Theory layer was reserved for multi-robot coordination, whereas the formal scope
centered on a single robot.

1.5 Refined Mid-Term Objectives

To make progress demonstrable and testable, the first phase is evaluated using these objectives:

1. Define shared spatial, sensor, state, and motor-command contracts.
2. Convert ultrasonic and ToF observations into an incremental occupancy grid.
3. Build deterministic safety and mission control using a priority Behavior Tree.
4. Provide a Gymnasium exploration environment and PPO training path without making RL responsible
   for safety.
5. Produce structured decision traces containing the selected action, reason, source layer,
   status, command, timestamp, and tick identifier.
6. Demonstrate navigation-decision integration using repeatable mock scenarios.
7. Preserve clear interfaces for WiFi detection, visualization, and eventual hardware publishers.

1.6 Scope

Included in the current software scope:

- One mobile robot in a 5 m x 5 m logical workspace represented by 50 x 50 cells.
- Incremental unknown/free/occupied mapping from five ultrasonic channels and an 8 x 8 ToF grid.
- Shared Blackboard communication.
- Behavior Tree safety, target confirmation, simple waypoint steering, exploration, and idle logic.
- Structured action explanations and deterministic integration scenarios.
- A custom RL environment and PPO training scaffold.
- Early isometric rendering and sensor-simulation prototypes.

Not yet included as verified mid-term functionality:

- Real CSI acquisition, preprocessing, trained CNN, localization, or accuracy results.
- A complete A* planner, frontier planner, or closed-loop navigation controller.
- Integrated fire, flood, chemical-leak, and collapse dynamics.
- GA optimization or comparative optimization results.
- Multi-robot coordination or Game Theory.
- Complete dashboard and live inter-module simulator.
- Motor/sensor firmware, physical robot, or HIL validation.
- Field rescue certification or safety guarantee.

1.7 Main Mid-Term Contribution

The main contribution at this stage is an evidence-backed, modular, and testable autonomy core. It
separates safety decisions from adaptive exploration and standardizes how independent team modules
exchange data. This foundation lowers integration risk and gives the final project measurable points
for later comparison.

2. LITERATURE REVIEW AND TECHNICAL BASIS
===============================================================================

2.1 Behavior Trees for Robotic Control

Behavior Trees organize control logic hierarchically and support modular, reactive task execution.
A priority selector can evaluate high-priority branches before lower-priority behavior and interrupt
lower branches when a safety condition becomes active. Literature identifies modularity,
reactivity, readability, and reuse as important reasons for their growing use in robotics [1,2].

Project Drishya uses a memoryless selector. On every tick it evaluates mission/data validity first,
then proximity safety, victim confirmation, target navigation, exploration, and idle fallback. This
ordering is central to the safety argument: a PPO action is not directly sent to motors without the
Behavior Tree being reevaluated at the next control tick. However, this is a software safety pattern,
not a formal proof of physical safety; real braking distance, communication delay, actuator failure,
and sensor uncertainty must still be evaluated.

2.2 Reinforcement Learning and PPO

RL learns a policy by maximizing expected cumulative reward through interaction with an
environment. PPO uses a clipped policy-update objective to limit overly large policy changes and is
widely used as a practical policy-gradient method [3]. The project defines a Gymnasium environment
with a 2,503-element observation (2,500 normalized occupancy values, normalized row and column,
and normalized victim distance), four discrete movement actions, rewards for exploration and victim
proximity, and penalties for collision and time.

This environment is currently a training scaffold. A meaningful final experiment must validate the
environment, control random seeds, train several independent runs, preserve checkpoints and logs,
compare against a deterministic heuristic, and report confidence intervals rather than a single best
episode. It must also avoid information leakage: the runtime robot cannot observe true victim
distance unless a real detection subsystem supplies an equivalent estimate.

2.3 Genetic Algorithms

GA methods are population-based search techniques suitable for non-differentiable, multi-parameter
optimization. The proposal assigns GA to high-level strategy or reward-weight optimization. That
layer is architecturally plausible but absent at mid-term. It should only be introduced after a
stable simulator and repeatable baseline exist; otherwise the optimizer would tune against a moving
implementation and produce results that cannot be interpreted.

2.4 Explainability

Explainability in this project is operational rather than purely visual. Each final command is
published with:

- tick identifier;
- selected behavior;
- human-readable reason;
- source layer (for example BT_SAFETY, BT_MISSION, RL_POLICY, or HEURISTIC_EXPLORATION);
- status;
- sanitized motor command; and
- timestamp.

This trace answers "which layer acted and why?" It does not fully explain the internal causal basis
of a neural policy. When PPO is integrated, the project should add policy confidence or action
distribution, observation snapshot/hash, model version, and safety-override events. The report
therefore distinguishes decision provenance from complete model interpretability.

2.5 Occupancy-Grid Mapping and Ray Casting

The project represents the map using FREE=0, OCCUPIED=1, and UNKNOWN=2. Sensor endpoints are
converted from robot-relative distance and angle into grid coordinates. Bresenham's integer line
algorithm enumerates cells along each sensor ray [11]. Cells before a valid detected endpoint are
marked free, while the endpoint is marked occupied. This is computationally simple and appropriate
for a first deterministic mapper.

The present map is categorical, not probabilistic. It does not model sensor likelihood, confidence,
false returns, pose uncertainty, temporal decay, or dynamic hazards. A log-odds occupancy update or
similar evidence-fusion method is recommended for the final system.

2.6 WiFi CSI Human Sensing

CSI captures per-subcarrier channel response information and is richer than a single RSSI value.
Espressif provides ESP32 CSI acquisition APIs and callback mechanisms [7]. Peer-reviewed work has
demonstrated feasibility for through-wall presence or activity sensing [8,9], but also documents
attenuation, multipath, environmental sensitivity, and domain generalization problems. Therefore,
the project must collect its own labeled data across people, locations, wall types, distances, and
days. Claims about victim detection must report both sensitivity and false-alarm behavior and must
use person-independent and environment-aware train/test splits.

2.7 A* and Frontier Exploration

A* searches for a least-cost path using accumulated path cost plus a heuristic estimate [10].
Frontier exploration selects boundaries between known free space and unknown space. Both are named
in the project architecture but are not implemented as complete navigation modules in the audited
repository. The current target behavior performs direct heading-based steering and the fallback
explorer counts unknown cells in short directional windows. These are useful baselines, not
substitutes for collision-aware global planning.

2.8 Related Mobile-Robot Navigation Work

Shrestha et al. reported mobile-robot navigation using ROS 2 Nav2, SLAM, and LiDAR [12]. This work
is especially relevant as a recent IEEE-published mobile-robot study involving authors from
Purwanchal Campus. It provides a useful local reference for integrating mapping, localization, and
navigation into a physical platform. Project Drishya differs in its present sensing and research
emphasis: it currently develops a lightweight ultrasonic/ToF occupancy mapper, WiFi CSI victim
perception, and an explainable BT/RL decision layer rather than a LiDAR-centered ROS 2 navigation
stack. The paper therefore informs the project's integration and hardware-validation approach
without being presented as evidence that Drishya itself has implemented Nav2, LiDAR, or SLAM.

2.9 Identified Research and Engineering Gap

Existing techniques individually address safety logic, adaptive exploration, mapping, or wireless
sensing. The project opportunity is their disciplined integration in an affordable platform with a
clear command provenance trail. The contribution should not be framed as inventing BT, PPO, GA, or
CSI. It should be framed as designing and evaluating a modular hybrid framework in which learned
adaptation remains subordinate to explicit safety and every control decision is auditable.

3.1 Functional Requirements

FR-01  The system shall accept validated ultrasonic and ToF sensor packets.
FR-02  The system shall maintain a shared coordinate convention across modules.
FR-03  The system shall incrementally classify grid cells as unknown, free, or occupied.
FR-04  The system shall publish state through a synchronized Blackboard.
FR-05  The decision layer shall stop when mission control or critical state is invalid/stale.
FR-06  The decision layer shall stop when a valid proximity reading is below 15 cm.
FR-07  A fresh detection at or above 0.85 confidence shall generate a bounded map waypoint.
FR-08  An active waypoint shall trigger navigation behavior until arrival.
FR-09  In the absence of danger and a target, the robot shall select an exploration action.
FR-10  The final command shall be bounded to the agreed motor envelope.
FR-11  Every published decision shall include an explanation trace.
FR-12  The simulator and later hardware shall use equivalent external packet contracts.

3.2 Non-Functional Requirements

NFR-01 Safety: deterministic stop conditions shall precede learning-based behavior.
NFR-02 Modularity: modules shall communicate through shared contracts, not private internals.
NFR-03 Testability: critical conversions, map updates, and tree nodes shall have automated tests.
NFR-04 Reproducibility: simulation scenarios and future ML experiments shall use recorded configs
       and random seeds.
NFR-05 Explainability: decision provenance shall be readable by a reviewer and parsable by software.
NFR-06 Timing: the decision loop targets 10 Hz in software; hardware timing remains to be measured.
NFR-07 Portability: the software shall run first on a development computer and later on Raspberry Pi.
NFR-08 Maintainability: constants and Blackboard keys shall be centralized and documented.

3.4 Coordinate System

- Physical origin: robot starting point (0 cm, 0 cm).
- Initial heading: 0 degrees along positive X.
- Positive Y: robot's left at the initial frame.
- Cell size: 10 cm.
- Grid dimensions: 50 rows x 50 columns.
- Physical origin maps to logical cell (row 25, column 25).
- world_to_grid: column = round(x/10)+25; row = round(-y/10)+25.
- grid_to_world applies the inverse convention at cell centers.

This convention must be displayed during the defense because row/column and X/Y confusion is a
common integration failure.

3.5 Sensor Packet Contract

Required fields are five non-negative integer ultrasonic distances (front, left 45 degrees, left
90 degrees, right 45 degrees, right 90 degrees), an 8 x 8 list of non-negative integer ToF values,
and a non-negative integer timestamp. A distance of zero represents unavailable/invalid data and is
ignored by mapping.

The final contract should add units, maximum range, no-return semantics, sensor identifier,
sequence number, schema version, timestamp clock definition, and checksum or transport validation.

3.6 Blackboard Contract

Important inputs include mission/control, navigation/robot_pose,
navigation/occupancy_grid, navigation/target_waypoint, sensor/proximity, and detection/result.
Important outputs include state/motor_command, state/bt_status, decision/state, and decision/trace.

Atomic update_many() and consistent deep-copy snapshots are available. Individual get() operations
return stored references, so callers must avoid mutating shared arrays or dictionaries outside the
lock. A final hardened version should either return immutable/copy-on-read payloads or formally
document ownership.

3.7 Behavior Tree Priority

SafetyGate -> EmergencyStop -> VictimConfirmation -> NavigateToTarget -> RLExplore -> Idle

The root is a memoryless Selector. A child that succeeds or is running prevents lower-priority
children from executing during that tick. The tree is evaluated at a nominal 10 Hz by Brain.

4. METHODOLOGY
===============================================================================

4.1 Development Strategy

The project follows simulation-first, contract-first, and modular development. Teams can build
against deterministic mock publishers before live modules are ready. Unit tests establish local
correctness, followed by headless cross-module scenarios. Closed-loop simulation and hardware are
subsequent integration stages.

4.2 Mapping Pipeline

1. Validate the sensor packet at the input boundary.
2. Read the robot pose and heading.
3. Convert each ultrasonic sensor direction into a global angle.
4. Convert the endpoint from centimeters to a grid cell.
5. Generate cells from robot to endpoint using Bresenham ray casting.
6. Mark intermediate valid cells free and a valid endpoint occupied.
7. Repeat for every cell in the 8 x 8 ToF array using the ToF projection function.
8. Publish a copy of the occupancy grid.

At mid-term, update_map() assumes it receives structurally valid input; the integration boundary
must explicitly call validation in the final runtime path.

4.3 Decision Pipeline

1. Publish mission, pose, map, proximity, and detection state.
2. Increment the decision tick identifier.
3. Reject missing, malformed, stale, unavailable, or paused state through SafetyGate.
4. Stop if any available proximity sensor is below 15 cm.
5. Confirm only a fresh detection with confidence >= 0.85 and valid in-map coordinates.
6. If a waypoint exists, compute target heading and issue bounded differential motor values.
7. Otherwise use PPO if a checkpoint loads; if it does not, use the deterministic heuristic.
8. If the map is fully explored or no action is possible, enter Idle.
9. Atomically publish command, state, status, and trace.

4.4 RL Environment

Each episode creates a 50 x 50 ground-truth grid with approximately 8% obstacle density, places the
robot at the center, and places a victim in a free cell away from the start. The action space is
forward, left, backward, or right. A local square region with range five cells is revealed around
the robot. The reward is:

R_t = newly_explored_cells(1.0) - 0.05 per step - 5.0 per collision
      + 50.0 when the robot comes within three Manhattan cells of the victim.

Episodes terminate on victim discovery and truncate at 500 steps. The training script configures
PPO with four vectorized environments. These design choices require later ablation and leakage
review; they are not validated hyperparameters at mid-term.

4.5 Proposed CSI Method for the Next Phase

1. Fix transmitter, receiver, channel, bandwidth, antenna arrangement, and packet rate.
2. Record raw complex CSI with synchronized labels and metadata.
3. Remove invalid subcarriers/packets and correct phase artifacts where appropriate.
4. Form amplitude and/or calibrated phase windows.
5. Split data by person, session, and environment before model fitting.
6. Begin with non-neural baselines; then compare a compact CNN or temporal model.
7. Calibrate probabilities and choose the confirmation threshold using validation data.
8. Report confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1, ROC/PR behavior, false alarms per minute, and
   inference latency.
9. Only attempt localization after stable presence detection is demonstrated.

4.6 Evaluation Philosophy

The final evaluation must compare ablations:

- deterministic baseline only;
- BT + heuristic exploration;
- BT + PPO;
- BT + PPO + GA-tuned parameters, if GA remains in scope;
- safety layer disabled only in simulation to quantify its effect;
- known versus unseen layouts, hazard configurations, and CSI environments.

Each result must state sample count, seed policy, mean, dispersion, failure cases, and hardware or
simulation context.

5.3 Decision Logic - Implemented with Mock Inputs

The Brain runs the tree in a daemon thread at a nominal 10 Hz or can be ticked manually for tests.
Implemented nodes are:

SafetyGate:
Stops for missing/invalid mission control, operator emergency request, inactive mission, invalid or
stale pose, invalid map shape/values, invalid waypoint, invalid/unavailable proximity, or stale
proximity. State freshness is limited to 1,500 ms with up to 1,000 ms future clock skew.

EmergencyStop:
Stops when any available ultrasonic distance is greater than zero and less than 15 cm.

VictimConfirmation:
Accepts a detection only when confidence is finite and within [0,1], timestamp is no older than
5,000 ms, confidence is at least 0.85, coordinates are finite, and the resulting waypoint is on the
map. Duplicate detections are not reconfirmed.

NavigateToTarget:
Uses direct geometric heading error and an arrival radius of two cells. It generates forward, left,
or right differential commands. It does not yet consume an obstacle-free A* path.

RLExplore:
Loads a PPO checkpoint if present. At the audit cutoff no checkpoint is available, so the active
implementation uses a deterministic directional unknown-cell heuristic.

Idle:
Provides the safe fallback when no higher-priority behavior is actionable.

All commands pass through centralized sanitation: left/right values are clamped to [-255,255],
duration is clamped to [0,1000] ms, and invalid values become zero.

5.4 Explainability - Implemented at Decision-Provenance Level

The current trace records tick_id, selected_action, reason, source_layer, status, sanitized command,
and timestamp_ms. The integration demo shows distinguishable sources such as BT_SAFETY,

- Sensor rays can update a bounded categorical occupancy grid.
- The Behavior Tree gives safety and mission validity higher priority than exploration.
- Stale or malformed critical state causes a safe stop.
- Detection confidence and freshness gates prevent arbitrary mock detections from becoming targets.
- Motor values are centrally bounded.
- Decisions expose their selected layer and reason.
- Navigation mapping and decision logic can exchange data through the same Blackboard in a
  deterministic headless harness.

6.5 What the Results Do Not Establish

The evidence does not yet support these claims:

- 91.8% rescue success or any other rescue-success percentage.
- 98.2-second mean rescue/execution time.
- 42 Hz Raspberry Pi inference or 100 Hz ESP32 sensor streaming.
- Better performance of PPO than a heuristic, because no trained checkpoint is present.
- Better performance from GA, because GA is absent.
- Real victim detection or localization accuracy, because detection inputs are mocks.
- Collision-free target navigation, because direct steering is not A* path planning.
- Multi-hazard robustness, because integrated hazard dynamics are absent.
- Hardware-in-the-loop stability, because hardware integration is pending.

6.6 Correction to the Proposal's Results Chapter

The proposal's Chapter 5 presents exact results in completed tense. No repository experiment,
dataset, log, model, or HIL artifact substantiates those numbers at this cutoff. For academic
integrity, those values must not be repeated as achieved mid-term results. If they were intended as
expected targets or illustrative placeholders, relabel them accordingly. If the team has external
evidence not stored in the repository, it must be supplied with raw logs, configurations, sample
counts, analysis code, and hardware setup before the claims can be reinstated.

6.7 Discussion

The strongest mid-term achievement is architectural discipline. Safety checks, state freshness,
bounded actuation, mock independence, and traceable decisions are appropriate foundations for a
high-risk robot. The test count is meaningful because it covers many edge cases, but it should not
be confused with system validation. Most tests are component-level tests with deterministic data.

The largest technical gap is closed-loop reality. The mapping module updates a grid, but no complete
controller changes robot pose in response to commands and then regenerates sensors through the
supported integration path. The victim detector is mocked, global path planning is missing, and the
RL environment has not produced benchmark evidence. Completing that loop is more valuable than
adding optional Game Theory or a complex GA prematurely.

7.3 Technical Limitations

Mapping:

- Positive readings are treated as obstacle endpoints; explicit max-range/no-hit semantics are
  missing.
- All 64 ToF cells are projected without confidence or field-of-view calibration evidence.
- The categorical grid can lock false occupied cells and lacks probabilistic evidence fusion.
- Pose uncertainty and moving obstacles are not modeled.

Navigation:

- Direct steering does not check whether the straight path crosses occupied cells.
- No A*, frontier extraction, local planner, or recovery behavior is complete.
- Arrival radius of two cells equals roughly 20 cm and requires physical validation.

Decision safety:

- A 15 cm threshold is a software constant, not yet derived from stopping distance.
- 10 Hz is a configured target, not measured worst-case control latency.
- Thread/process failure, communication loss, watchdog, and actuator feedback are not implemented.
- Safe stop commands have not been tested on real motors.

RL:

- The observation contains victim-distance information that may be unavailable in real exploration.
- The simulator's simple square reveal differs from physical ultrasonic/ToF sensing.
- Random independent obstacle cells may not resemble traversable collapsed structures.
- No training, checkpoint, seed study, baseline, or generalization result exists.

WiFi CSI:

- No local dataset or implemented pipeline exists.
- Static victims, rubble composition, multipath, environmental change, and false positives are hard.
- Presence detection and localization are separate difficulty levels and should be staged.

Software integration:

- Documentation and actual files differ in places.
- Timestamp field names are in migration (timestamp and timestamp_ms).
- Blackboard get() can expose mutable references.
- The simulator is not yet connected to the supported queue/Blackboard loop.

7.5 Ethical and Safety Considerations

The robot should be presented as a decision-support prototype, not a replacement for trained rescue
personnel. A false negative may miss a victim; a false positive may redirect scarce rescue effort.
CSI sensing can also raise privacy concerns because it is device-free and non-visual. The final work
should document consent for human data collection, anonymize datasets, limit retention, state
failure modes, and preserve operator stop/override. Physical tests should begin at low speed in a
controlled enclosure with accessible power isolation.

10. CONCLUSION
===============================================================================

Project Drishya has established a credible mid-term software foundation for an explainable
autonomous SAR robot. The shared coordinate system, sensor contract, Blackboard, occupancy mapping,
priority Behavior Tree, safe command publication, decision trace, RL environment, and headless
integration harness are implemented. The audited suite passes 97 tests, and deterministic scenarios
show that mission validity and proximity safety override exploration, fresh high-confidence mock
detections become navigation targets, and each command carries a reason and source layer.

The project is not yet an end-to-end rescue robot. The current detector is mocked, target steering
is not a complete path planner, the simulator is not closed loop, PPO is untrained, GA is absent,
multi-hazard evaluation is incomplete, and hardware performance is unmeasured. Recognizing these
limits strengthens rather than weakens the defense: the team can demonstrate exactly what works,
show the tests, explain the architecture, and present a dependency-aware plan for the remaining
research.

The next decisive milestone is not an additional optional algorithm. It is a repeatable closed-loop
system in which sensing updates the map, the map supports a safe plan, the decision tree selects and
explains an action, the action changes the robot state, and the cycle is evaluated using fixed
metrics. Once this loop and the CSI baseline are stable, PPO and optional GA can be evaluated fairly.

APPENDIX C: LIKELY VIVA QUESTIONS AND MODEL ANSWERS
===============================================================================

Q1. What is the novelty of the project if BT, PPO, and CSI already exist?

The intended contribution is system integration and evaluation: an affordable SAR architecture in
which deterministic safety supervises adaptive exploration, WiFi CSI supplies non-visual detection,
and every command includes a provenance trace. We are not claiming to invent the component methods.

Q2. Why use a Behavior Tree instead of a finite-state machine?

The hierarchical priority structure is easier to extend and inspect, and a memoryless selector
reevaluates high-priority safety on every tick. A finite-state machine could implement the same
logic, but transitions become harder to maintain as behaviors and interruptions grow.

Q3. Is the Behavior Tree formally safe?

No. It enforces explicit software priorities and fail-safe validation, which is necessary but not a
formal physical safety proof. Braking distance, latency, sensor failure, watchdogs, and actuator
behavior still require hardware testing.

Q4. Why is RL not used for emergency avoidance?

Emergency behavior must be predictable and auditable. RL is restricted to exploration proposals;
deterministic branches retain authority over mission validity and immediate proximity hazards.

Q5. Are you currently using PPO?

The environment and training/inference path exist, but no trained checkpoint is present at the
mid-term cutoff. The running demo correctly identifies and uses a deterministic heuristic fallback.

Q6. Why use PPO?

PPO is a practical policy-gradient baseline with stable clipped updates and mature tooling. Its
suitability is a hypothesis to test against frontier and deterministic baselines, not an assumption.

Q7. What is wrong with the current RL observation?

It includes normalized true victim distance, which may be privileged information during exploration.
Before training, we must replace it with observable detector-derived information or remove it.

Q8. How is explainability achieved?

Each final decision records the tick, selected behavior, reason, source layer, status, sanitized
command, and timestamp. This provides decision provenance. For PPO, fuller interpretation would
also require model/version and action-distribution information.

Q9. Why use a Blackboard?

It decouples modules and allows separate developers or threads to exchange state through defined
keys. Atomic grouped updates and consistent snapshots reduce partial-state problems.

Q10. Is the Blackboard completely thread-safe?

Dictionary operations are lock-protected, and snapshots are deep copied. However, get() returns the
stored object reference, so external mutation is a remaining ownership risk that we plan to harden.

Q11. How does occupancy mapping work?

The robot pose and sensor angle define a grid endpoint. Bresenham enumerates cells along the ray;
intermediate cells are marked free and a valid endpoint occupied. Five ultrasonic rays and 64 ToF
projections update the map.

Q12. Why is a 15 cm emergency threshold used?

It is a conservative development constant for mock tests, not yet a physically validated threshold.
The final value must be derived from robot speed, sensing/control latency, braking distance, and a
safety margin.

Q13. Does the current navigator avoid obstacles on the way to a victim?

Not yet globally. The current behavior steers geometrically toward a waypoint while the emergency
branch handles immediate proximity. A* with obstacle inflation and replanning is remaining work.

Q14. Can WiFi really detect a person through rubble?

CSI-based non-line-of-sight sensing is supported by prior research, but performance is environment-
dependent. Our project must validate its own hardware and conditions. We will report limitations,
false positives, false negatives, and generalization rather than assume universal penetration.

Q15. Why not rely only on a camera or thermal camera?

Those sensors are valuable but can be blocked by occlusion. CSI is being studied as a complementary
modality. A field system would ideally fuse modalities rather than claim one sensor is sufficient.

Q16. How will CSI data be split for machine learning?

We will separate people, collection sessions, and preferably environments between training and test
sets. Randomly splitting adjacent windows from one recording would leak environmental patterns and
overstate accuracy.

Q17. Why is GA not implemented yet?

Optimization requires a stable simulator, baseline, and objective. Implementing GA before those
exist would optimize noise. It is sequenced after closed-loop evaluation and may be deferred if it
threatens core-system quality.

Q18. Why is Game Theory excluded?

The scoped platform is single-robot. Game Theory is useful for multi-robot allocation, but it is an
optional future extension and not necessary to prove the current research question.

Q19. What do 97 passing tests prove?

They provide reproducible evidence for component and Behavior Tree behavior under tested inputs.
They do not prove real-world rescue performance, CSI accuracy, or hardware safety.

Q20. Why do the proposal's result numbers not appear as achieved results here?

We could not identify raw data, experiment configuration, code artifacts, or hardware logs that
support them at the audit cutoff. To preserve academic integrity, we treat them as unverified
targets unless evidence is supplied.

Q21. What is the most important next milestone?

A supported closed-loop simulator: motor command changes pose, pose changes sensor readings,
sensors update the map, planning updates intent, and the Behavior Tree produces the next explained
command.

Q22. How will you show that the hybrid system is better?

Through controlled ablation: heuristic only, BT plus heuristic, BT plus PPO, and optionally GA-tuned
variants, all evaluated on identical held-out scenarios with repeated seeds and confidence intervals.

Q23. What happens when sensors stop publishing?

Critical pose and proximity payloads have freshness limits. Stale or missing critical state triggers
SafetyGate and produces a zero motor command. Hardware communication timeout/watchdog remains to be
implemented.

Q24. What is your primary limitation at mid-term?

The components are not yet connected in a complete closed loop, and the victim detector remains a
mock. Therefore current evidence is software-foundation evidence rather than end-to-end performance.

Q25. If PPO performs worse than the heuristic, is the project a failure?

No. A rigorous negative comparison is an engineering result. The safe modular architecture retains
the heuristic, and the analysis would identify why the learned policy failed and what conditions it
requires. We should select the method supported by evidence.
